\section{Evaluation Settings}

We evaluate on two settings, and both exclude the augmented family of Section~4.2. Setting~A, the
mixed setting, holds the 1,823 as-published, cross-document, and synthesized cases. Setting~B holds
the 1,000 synthesized cases alone, whose answer sets are the largest and whose retrieval is
therefore the hardest.

Excluding the augmented cases matters enough to justify. When we include them, the baseline rises
from 0.521 to 0.622 while the proposed method rises from 0.743 to 0.778, so the apparent gap shrinks
from $+0.222$ to $+0.156$.
% source: experiments/strat_full.json（baseline 0.6218, reranker-10S 0.7777）vs strat_A.json
%         （baseline 0.5206, reranker-10S 0.7429）
Perturbed copies of a case are easy, because a paraphrase leaves the variables untouched and a
variable swap leaves the description untouched; including them measures the augmentation rather than
the method. Every result in Chapter~6 uses the stratified source-disjoint split of Section~4.4 with
the same 10 random seeds.

\section{Compared Methods}

We compare the proposed method against one internal and one external baseline. The internal baseline
is classical information retrieval: it ranks the whole database by the Stage-1 score of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stage1}, without a learned model and without a candidate window. The proposed method
is reranker-10S, which reranks the top $k = 50$ candidates with the ten features of
Table~\ref{tab:features}; reranker-7, which drops the three set-aware features, isolates their
contribution. Sections~\ref{sec:greedyresults} and \ref{sec:dofstopresults} add the two sequential variants and the self-terminating variant
of Sections~\ref{sec:seq} and \ref{sec:dofstop}.

The external baseline asks a large language model to write the equations directly. Given the same
query, the model generates the equations it judges necessary; a second, more capable model then
decides which generated equation is mathematically equivalent to which ground-truth equation, and
we score the resulting matches with the same metrics. Equivalence judging is expensive, so this
comparison uses a uniform sample of 48 cases in Setting~A and 50 cases in Setting~B.

Because the judge is a different model from the generator, the equivalence decision does not
depend on the generator recognizing its own phrasing. The comparison still has limits --- about
50 cases per setting, machine-judged equivalence, and prompts we did not tune --- and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} records them.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}

The primary metric matches the evaluation window to the answer size of each case. For a query with
ground truth $\mathcal{R}_q$ we take the top $|\mathcal{R}_q|$ equations of the ranking and report
the fraction of $\mathcal{R}_q$ they contain,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Recall}@K_{\mathrm{correct}}(q)
    = \frac{|\mathcal{R}_q \cap \mathrm{Top}_{|\mathcal{R}_q|}(q)|}{|\mathcal{R}_q|} ,
  \label{eq:rk}
\end{equation}
averaged over cases. Because the window equals the answer size, precision and recall coincide, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rk} is the $R$-precision of the information-retrieval literature.
% source: evaluate_multi_eq.py case_metrics（recall_at_n_correct = precision_at_C, R-Precision）
A fixed window would distort the comparison across our case families: Recall@3 caps a case with
eight ground-truth equations at $3/8$ and rewards a case with one ground-truth equation for two
wrong guesses, and our answer sizes range from 1 to 10. We report mean average precision (MAP) and
Recall@20 as secondary metrics, the latter because a human reviewing 20 candidates is a realistic
use of the method.

Self-terminating retrieval returns a set rather than a ranking, so we score it with set-level
measures: the exact-match rate of the returned set against $\mathcal{R}_q$, the set $F_1$, and the
closure rate, which is the fraction of returned sets that satisfy the closure test of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:closed}. We compare DoF-stop against an oracle that is told the answer size and takes
the top $|\mathcal{R}_q|$ equations.

One asymmetry between the compared methods deserves a note. The baseline ranks the whole database, so
nothing bounds its recall, whereas every reranker sees only the $k$ candidates of Stage~1 and cannot
exceed their coverage --- 0.920 at $k = 50$ (Section~\ref{sec:stage1}). The reported gains are
therefore gains despite a handicap, and Section~\ref{sec:erroranalysis} shows that the candidate window, not
the reranker, causes most of the residual error.

\section{Implementation and Reproducibility}

Every experiment runs as a deterministic script: the random seed fixes the split, the model
initialization, and the negative sampling, so a rerun reproduces the reported numbers exactly. The
proposed method itself calls no language model at inference time. Language models appear only in
building the data and in the external baseline --- extraction (Gemini 2.5 Pro), the descriptions of
the synthesized cases, and the generation and equivalence judging of the external baseline --- and
each call pins a model identifier.

\section{Statistical Testing}

We test paired differences over the 10 seeds with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and report the
paired $t$-test as a reference. Each seed contributes one pair, because both methods run on the same
split. We report the effect size (Cohen's $d_z$) beside every $p$-value, and we call a difference
significant only after such a test. With $n = 10$ pairs the smallest two-sided Wilcoxon $p$-value is
0.00195, which a method attains when it wins on all 10 seeds; we therefore read 0.00195 as ``every
seed agreed'' rather than as a precise probability.
% source: experiments/significance_stats.json（p_wilcoxon 0.00195 = n=10 の理論最小値）
The tests cover the baseline against the proposed method in both settings, the three selection
strategies against one another, and DoF-stop against the oracle that knows the answer size.
