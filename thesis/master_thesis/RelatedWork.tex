\section{Component Technologies for Automated Physical Model Building}
\label{sec:rw-autopmob}

Our group proposed AutoPMoB, an automated physical model builder that collects documents about a
target process, extracts equations, variables, and experimental data from them, judges the
equivalence of the extracted information across documents, and reorganizes the information into
the desired model\cite{AutoPMoBCSTR,AutoPMoBProto}. Its component technologies have been developed
step by step: an extraction algorithm with an annotation tool (VARAT) collects variable symbols
from engineering documents\cite{VARAT}, a data-augmentation method generates training sentences
from templates to extract variable definitions\cite{Nagayama}, ProcessBERT --- a language model
pre-trained on a chemical-engineering corpus --- judges whether two variable definitions in
different documents refer to the same quantity\cite{ProcessBERT}, and computer-algebra algorithms
judge whether two groups of equations that represent process dynamics, such as
differential-algebraic equation systems, are mathematically equivalent\cite{ZhangEquiv,DAEEquiv}.

The work closest to this study addresses the model-building step itself. Kato and Kano proposed an
efficient algorithm that builds a physical model from equations extracted from
documents\cite{KatoESCAPE}. Because such equations include redundant, intermediate, and
inconsistent ones, they later formulated model building as the combination of extracted equations
into models that satisfy four predefined requirements, such as input--output completeness and
consistency, and proposed an algorithm that finds every desired model while avoiding unnecessary
computation; on eight case studies, it built all the desired models\cite{CombineEq}.

This study differs from the combination method in its use of context. The combination method
builds models from the equations and their variables; this study also reads the context of each
equation --- the description of the desired model and the text that surrounds the equation in its
source document. Context lets the method narrow a database of 11,146 equations drawn from 361
sources to the equations that concern the requested model, and the variables that the equations
share then decide which of them form the model (Section~\ref{sec:nullresult}). Along the way, the
method evaluates each candidate against the equations already selected and stops when the selected
set closes as a system (Chapter~3).

\section{Equation Discovery from Numerical Data}
\label{sec:rw-sr}

Symbolic regression finds a symbolic expression that matches data from an unknown
function\cite{AIFeynman}. Schmidt and Lipson distilled Hamiltonians, Lagrangians, and conservation
laws from motion-tracking data of physical systems without prior knowledge of
physics\cite{SchmidtLipson}; SINDy recovers governing equations by sparse regression over a library
of candidate terms\cite{SINDy}; AI Feynman combines neural-network fitting with physics-inspired
techniques and rediscovered all 100 equations it was given from the Feynman Lectures on
Physics\cite{AIFeynman}; and ALAMO learns algebraic surrogate models for process systems from
simulation or experimental data\cite{ALAMO}. Such methods largely extract equations from data alone
and often neglect the domain knowledge that scientists rely on\cite{LLMSR}. LLM-SR fills this gap:
a large language model proposes equation skeletons --- equations whose parameters are left
undetermined --- from its scientific knowledge, and an optimizer fits the parameters to the
data\cite{LLMSR}.

This study differs from symbolic regression in where its equations come from. Symbolic regression
derives a model from numerical data; this study builds a model from equations published in the
literature, so it needs no data measured on the target process, and every equation it returns
keeps its source document. Unlike conventional symbolic regression, which reads numbers alone, the
method also reads context, the description of the desired model and the text around each
equation. The two approaches can also work together. The equations that this study retrieves, with
their coefficients left free, can serve as candidate skeletons for symbolic regression; unlike the
skeletons that a language model proposes in LLM-SR, they carry their sources.

\section{Information Retrieval and Reranking}
\label{sec:rw-ir}

Classical information retrieval ranks the items of a collection by their relevance to a
query\cite{Manning}. Term-weighting schemes such as term frequency--inverse document frequency
(TF-IDF) score the lexical overlap between query and item\cite{Salton}, the probabilistic BM25
family refines such scores\cite{BM25}, and latent semantic analysis projects texts onto a
low-dimensional space obtained by singular-value decomposition to absorb vocabulary
mismatch\cite{LSA}. Modern systems add a second stage: a fast first stage narrows the collection
to a candidate list, and a learned model reranks the candidates, today typically a pretrained
language model such as BERT\cite{BERT,BERTrerank}; dense retrievers replace the lexical first
stage with learned embeddings\cite{DPR}. The method of Chapter~3 inherits this retrieve-then-rerank
design --- its first stage mixes a TF-IDF score with a variable-overlap score, its reranker trains
with a pairwise ranking loss from the learning-to-rank literature\cite{LTR}, and its
latent-similarity feature applies latent semantic analysis.

These methods score each item independently of the other retrieved items. The best-known departure
is maximal marginal relevance (MMR), which penalizes a candidate for resembling the items already
selected and thereby diversifies the result list\cite{MMR}. Our task needs a different relation.
A physical model is a system of equations, so a good candidate should \emph{connect} to the
equations already selected --- share their variables --- and supply the query variables they still
miss. Where MMR treats overlap with the selected items as redundancy, equation-set retrieval treats
shared variables as the links that make the equations a system, because it must achieve
solvability rather than diversity. The set-aware features of Chapter~3 measure exactly these two
relations.

\section{Direct Generation of Domain Knowledge by Large Language Models}
\label{sec:rw-llm}

Large language models (LLMs) generate domain knowledge directly from a natural-language
prompt\cite{GPT3}, and they already propose equation skeletons for symbolic regression
(Section~\ref{sec:rw-sr}). Asking an LLM to write out the equations of the desired model is
therefore a natural alternative to retrieving them from a database.

Generation, however, carries no provenance. A generated equation can be hallucinated --- fluent
but unsupported by any source\cite{Hallucination} --- and even a correct one may arrive in a
notation that matches no document, while empirical correlations whose coefficients were fitted to
data are hard to reproduce exactly. This study therefore treats direct generation as an external
baseline (Chapter~5) and retrieves equations instead: every returned equation exists in a source
document that an engineer can audit, and inference runs a small multilayer perceptron without
calling an LLM. LLMs still serve in data construction: one extracts the database equations from
documents (Chapter~4), and another writes only the natural-language descriptions of the
synthesized cases, whose answer sets come from the database itself (Section~4.2).

\section{Mathematical Information Retrieval}
\label{sec:rw-mathir}

Mathematical information retrieval searches for formulas with queries that contain text, formulas,
or both, and its recognition and retrieval techniques have been surveyed\cite{MathSurvey}. Shared
tasks organized the field: the NTCIR-12 MathIR task evaluated formula-and-keyword search over
arXiv and Wikipedia corpora\cite{NTCIR12}, and the ARQMath lab evaluates the retrieval of answers
and of formulas for mathematical questions from a community-question-answering
archive\cite{ARQMath}. Retrieval systems of this field rank individual formulas, for example by
matching leaf-root paths in the operator subtrees of a formula\cite{Approach0}.

The unit of retrieval separates this study from formula search. A formula-search system returns
single formulas ranked by similarity to the query; our method returns a set of equations that must
jointly close a system --- cover the requested outputs and leave zero degrees of freedom --- so
the value of one equation depends on the equations retrieved with it. No formula-search benchmark
imposes this joint condition. Chapter~6 shows its consequence empirically: after the first stage
narrows the candidates, topical similarity becomes redundant, and the improvement comes from
features that read the variable structure of the selected set.
