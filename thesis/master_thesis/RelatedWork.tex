\section{Component Technologies for Automated Physical Model Building}
\label{sec:rw-autopmob}

Our group proposed AutoPMoB, an automated physical model builder that collects documents about a
target process, extracts equations, variables, and experimental data from them, judges the
equivalence of the extracted information across documents, and reorganizes the information into
the desired model\cite{AutoPMoBCSTR,AutoPMoBProto}. Its component technologies have been developed
step by step: an extraction algorithm with an annotation tool (VARAT) collects variable symbols
from engineering documents\cite{VARAT}, ProcessBERT --- a language model pre-trained on a
chemical-engineering corpus --- judges whether two variable definitions in different documents
refer to the same quantity\cite{ProcessBERT}, and a computer-algebra algorithm judges whether two
differential-algebraic equation systems are mathematically equivalent\cite{DAEEquiv}. Closest to
this study, Kato and Kano build a model by combining extracted equations that satisfy four
predefined requirements\cite{CombineEq}.

A separate line of work identifies a model from measured data instead of documents: SINDy recovers
governing equations by sparse regression over a library of candidate terms\cite{SINDy}, and ALAMO
learns algebraic surrogate models for process systems from simulation or experimental
data\cite{ALAMO}. These methods need data measured on the target process and search a space of
functional forms, whereas the literature route reuses equations that experts have already
published, together with the documents they came from.

The works above either extract and align the information a model builder needs or combine it by
requirements fixed in advance. This study addresses the step in between as a learning problem: it
learns to rank the equations of an 11,146-equation database so that the top of the ranking forms
the requested model, it evaluates each candidate against the equations already selected, and it
stops when the selected set closes as a system (Chapter~3).

\section{Information Retrieval and Reranking}
\label{sec:rw-ir}

Classical information retrieval ranks the items of a collection by their relevance to a
query\cite{Manning}. Term-weighting schemes such as term frequency--inverse document frequency
(TF-IDF) score the lexical overlap between query and item\cite{Salton}, the probabilistic BM25
family refines such scores\cite{BM25}, and latent semantic analysis projects texts onto a
low-dimensional space obtained by singular-value decomposition to absorb vocabulary
mismatch\cite{LSA}. Modern systems add a second stage: a fast first stage narrows the collection
to a candidate list, and a learned model reranks the candidates, today typically a pretrained
language model such as BERT\cite{BERT,BERTrerank}; dense retrievers replace the lexical first
stage with learned embeddings\cite{DPR}. The method of Chapter~3 inherits this retrieve-then-rerank
design --- its first stage mixes a TF-IDF score with a variable-overlap score, its reranker trains
with a pairwise ranking loss from the learning-to-rank literature\cite{LTR}, and its
latent-similarity feature applies latent semantic analysis.

These methods score each item independently of the other retrieved items. The best-known departure
is maximal marginal relevance (MMR), which penalizes a candidate for resembling the items already
selected and thereby diversifies the result list\cite{MMR}. Our task departs in the opposite
direction: a physical model is a system of equations, so a good candidate should \emph{connect} to
the equations already selected --- share their variables --- and supply the query variables they
still miss. The set-aware features of Chapter~3 reward exactly this coupling, with the opposite
sign of MMR's penalty, because equation-set retrieval must achieve solvability rather than
diversity.

\section{Direct Generation of Domain Knowledge by Large Language Models}
\label{sec:rw-llm}

Large language models (LLMs) generate domain knowledge directly from a natural-language
prompt\cite{GPT3}, and recent work applies them to scientific equations: LLM-SR, for example,
treats equations as programs and searches equation skeletons by combining an LLM's scientific
prior knowledge with evolutionary refinement\cite{LLMSR}. Asking an LLM to write out the equations
of the desired model is therefore a natural alternative to retrieving them from a database.

Generation, however, carries no provenance. A generated equation can be hallucinated --- fluent
but unsupported by any source\cite{Hallucination} --- and even a correct one may arrive in a
notation that matches no document, while empirical correlations whose coefficients were fitted to
data are hard to reproduce exactly. This study therefore treats direct generation as an external
baseline (Chapter~5) and retrieves equations instead: every returned equation exists in a source
document that an engineer can audit, and inference runs a small multilayer perceptron without
calling an LLM. LLMs still serve in data construction: one extracts the database equations from
documents (Chapter~4), and another writes only the natural-language descriptions of the
synthesized cases, whose answer sets come from the database itself (Section~4.2).

\section{Mathematical Information Retrieval}
\label{sec:rw-mathir}

Mathematical information retrieval searches for formulas with queries that contain text, formulas,
or both, and its recognition and retrieval techniques have been surveyed\cite{MathSurvey}. Shared
tasks organized the field: the NTCIR-12 MathIR task evaluated formula-and-keyword search over
arXiv and Wikipedia corpora\cite{NTCIR12}, and the ARQMath lab evaluates the retrieval of answers
and of formulas for mathematical questions from a community-question-answering
archive\cite{ARQMath}. Retrieval systems of this field rank individual formulas, for example by
matching leaf-root paths in the operator subtrees of a formula\cite{Approach0}.

The unit of retrieval separates this study from formula search. A formula-search system returns
single formulas ranked by similarity to the query; our method returns a set of equations that must
jointly close a system --- cover the requested outputs and leave zero degrees of freedom --- so
the value of one equation depends on the equations retrieved with it. No formula-search benchmark
imposes this joint condition. Chapter~6 shows its consequence empirically: after the first stage
narrows the candidates, topical similarity becomes redundant, and the improvement comes from
features that read the variable structure of the selected set.
