\section{Component Technologies for Automated Physical Model Building}
\label{sec:rw-autopmob}

Our group proposed AutoPMoB, an automated physical model builder that collects documents about a
target process, extracts equations, variables, and experimental data from them, judges the
equivalence of the extracted information across documents, and reorganizes the information into
the desired model\cite{AutoPMoBCSTR,AutoPMoBProto}. Its component technologies have been developed
step by step. For extraction, an algorithm with an annotation tool, the Variable Annotation Tool
(VARAT), collects variable symbols from engineering documents\cite{VARAT}, and a data-augmentation
method generates training sentences from templates to extract variable definitions\cite{Nagayama}.
For equivalence judgment, ProcessBERT --- a language model pre-trained on a chemical-engineering
corpus --- judges whether two variable definitions in different documents refer to the same
quantity\cite{ProcessBERT}, and two algorithms judge whether two groups of equations are
equivalent: one for groups that represent process dynamics\cite{ZhangEquiv} and one for
differential-algebraic equation systems\cite{DAEEquiv}. Both manipulate the equations with a
computer algebra system; the former judges equivalence by the calculations the equations perform
rather than by their appearance, and the latter calls two equation groups equivalent when they can
be transformed into the same form.

The work closest to this study addresses the model-building step itself. Kato and Kano proposed an
efficient algorithm that builds a physical model from equations extracted from
documents\cite{KatoESCAPE}. Because such equations include redundant, intermediate, and
inconsistent ones, they later formulated model building as the combination of extracted equations
into models that satisfy four predefined requirements, such as input--output completeness and
consistency, and proposed an algorithm that finds every desired model while avoiding unnecessary
computation; on eight case studies, it built all the desired models\cite{CombineEq}.

This study differs from the combination method in its input and output. The combination method
takes equations and their variables and returns every combination that meets the requirements.
This study also takes a description of the desired model, reads the context of each equation ---
the text that surrounds the equation in its source --- and ranks the equations that the
description asks for above the rest; it scores each candidate against the equations already
selected and can stop once the selected set closes as a system (Chapter~3). Narrowing the database
to the equations that concern the requested model, however, does not by itself require context. In
our benchmark, a first stage that reads variable overlap alone keeps 96.1\% of the ground-truth
equations among its 50 candidates, and adding text similarity raises this only to 96.7\%
(Section~\ref{sec:stage1redesign}); after narrowing, the features that read variable overlap
carry the ranking (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). The benchmark favors variables, because every
query takes its variables from the symbols of its ground-truth equations. Context should matter
where variables cannot decide --- when several combinations meet the requirements and only the
description tells which one the user wants, or when a user writes variables in a notation that
differs from the literature --- and this study has not yet shown that it helps there
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Equation Discovery from Numerical Data}
\label{sec:rw-sr}

Symbolic regression searches for an equation that fits numerical data, determining the form of the
equation as well as its coefficients\cite{AIFeynman}. Schmidt and Lipson distilled Hamiltonians,
Lagrangians, and conservation laws from motion-tracking data of physical systems without prior
knowledge of physics\cite{SchmidtLipson}; sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy)
uses sparse regression to keep the fewest terms of the governing equations that represent the
data\cite{SINDy}; AI Feynman combines neural-network fitting with physics-inspired techniques and
rediscovered all 100 equations it was given from the Feynman Lectures on Physics\cite{AIFeynman};
and automated learning of algebraic models for optimization (ALAMO) learns algebraic surrogate
models that are as accurate and as simple as possible from a small number of simulations or
experiments\cite{ALAMO}. The difficulty lies in the search: the candidate forms of an equation
span an extremely large combinatorial space, and methods that learn from data alone leave unused
the prior knowledge that scientists rely on\cite{LLMSR}. LLM-SR brings that knowledge in. A large
language model (LLM) proposes equation skeletons --- equations whose parameters are left
undetermined --- from its scientific knowledge, and an optimizer fits the parameters to the data;
with this prior, LLM-SR explored the space more efficiently than symbolic-regression baselines and
found more accurate equations, particularly on out-of-domain test data\cite{LLMSR}.

This study differs from symbolic regression in where its equations come from. Symbolic regression
derives a model from numerical data; this study builds a model from equations collected from the
literature, so it needs no data measured on the target process, and the equations that come from
documents --- 94\% of the database --- keep their sources. Unlike conventional symbolic regression,
which reads numbers alone, the method also reads context, the description of the desired model and
the text around each equation. The two approaches can also work together. The equations that this
study retrieves, with their coefficients left free, can serve as candidate skeletons for symbolic
regression; unlike the skeletons that an LLM proposes in LLM-SR, those drawn from documents carry
their sources.

\section{Information Retrieval and Reranking}
\label{sec:rw-ir}

Classical information retrieval ranks the items of a collection by their relevance to a
query\cite{Manning}. Term-weighting schemes such as term frequency--inverse document frequency
(TF-IDF) score the lexical overlap between query and item\cite{Salton}, the probabilistic BM25
family refines such scores\cite{BM25}, and latent semantic analysis projects texts onto a
low-dimensional space obtained by singular-value decomposition to absorb vocabulary
mismatch\cite{LSA}. Modern systems add a second stage: a fast first-stage retriever narrows the
collection to a candidate list, and a learned model reranks the candidates, today typically a
pretrained language model such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers
(BERT)\cite{BERT,BERTrerank}; dense retrievers perform the first-stage retrieval with learned
embeddings in place of lexical scores\cite{DPR}. The method of Chapter~3 inherits this
retrieve-then-rerank design --- its first stage mixes a TF-IDF score with a variable-overlap score,
its reranker trains with a pairwise ranking loss from the learning-to-rank literature\cite{LTR},
and its latent-similarity feature applies latent semantic analysis.

These methods score each item independently of the other retrieved items. The best-known departure
is maximal marginal relevance (MMR), which penalizes a candidate for resembling the items already
selected and thereby diversifies the result list\cite{MMR}. Our task needs a different relation.
A physical model is a system of equations, so a good candidate should \emph{connect} to the
equations already selected --- share their variables --- and supply the query variables they still
miss. Where MMR treats overlap with the selected items as redundancy, equation-set retrieval treats
shared variables as the links that make the equations a system, because it must achieve
solvability rather than diversity. The set-aware features of Chapter~3 measure exactly these two
relations.

\section{Direct Generation of Domain Knowledge by Large Language Models}
\label{sec:rw-llm}

LLMs generate domain knowledge directly from a natural-language prompt\cite{GPT3}, and they already
propose equation skeletons for symbolic regression (Section~\ref{sec:rw-sr}). Asking an LLM to
write out the equations of the desired model is therefore a natural alternative to retrieving them
from a database.

Generation, however, carries no provenance. LLMs are well known to hallucinate --- to produce
fluent output that no source supports\cite{Hallucination} --- and a user cannot trace a generated
equation to a source to check whether it is correct. The check matters for physical models: the
equations are solved as one system, so a single wrong equation can corrupt every variable connected
to it. This study therefore treats direct generation as an external baseline (Chapter~5) and
retrieves equations instead: a user can check each equation drawn from a document --- 94\% of the
database --- against its source, and inference runs a small multilayer perceptron without calling
an LLM. LLMs still serve in data construction (Chapter~4): one extracted the equations from
documents, generated the remaining 6\% as reference sets, which therefore have no source document,
and picked the answer sets of the 92 single-source cases; others wrote 33 of the cross-source
combinations and the natural-language descriptions of the synthesized cases, whose answer sets
follow from a rule.

\section{Mathematical Information Retrieval}
\label{sec:rw-mathir}

Mathematical information retrieval searches for formulas with queries that contain text, formulas,
or both, and its recognition and retrieval techniques have been surveyed\cite{MathSurvey}. We relate
the field to this study from two viewpoints: how it compares formulas and what it retrieves.

Formula-search methods compare formulas in several ways. Some treat a formula as a string of
symbols; others represent it as an operator tree and match the leaf-root paths of its
subtrees\cite{Approach0}, or embed its trees into vectors\cite{TangentCFT}. For this study, such
methods are components rather than rivals. Our method also compares equations --- the second stage
scores each candidate against the equations already selected --- but it reads only the sets of
variables that the equations contain, a naive comparison. Whether the tree-based or embedding
representations of formula search improve retrieval is left to future work (Chapter~7).

What the field retrieves separates it from this study. Shared tasks organized the field: the
NTCIR-12 MathIR task, held at the NII Testbeds and Community for Information access Research
(NTCIR) conference, evaluated formula-and-keyword search over arXiv and Wikipedia
corpora\cite{NTCIR12}, and the Answer Retrieval for Questions on Math (ARQMath) lab evaluates the
retrieval of answers and of formulas for mathematical questions from a
community-question-answering archive\cite{ARQMath}. These tasks retrieve single formulas ranked by
similarity to the query. Our method retrieves a set of equations that must jointly close a system
--- cover the requested outputs and leave zero degrees of freedom --- so the value of one equation
depends on the equations retrieved with it. We found no formula-search benchmark that imposes this
joint condition; retrieving several equations as one answer is a different problem from retrieving
a single formula and calls for its own task definition. Chapter~6 shows a consequence of the joint
condition: after the first stage narrows the candidates, topical similarity becomes redundant, and
the improvement comes from features that read the variable structure of the selected set.
