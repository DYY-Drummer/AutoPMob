Physical models --- systems of equations that describe how the material, energy, and momentum
of a process change --- underpin the process industry. Process design sizes equipment on them,
process control tunes controllers on them, and a digital twin keeps such a model in step with the
running plant\cite{DigitalTwin}. Purpose-built tools such as MATLAB/Simulink\cite{simulink} and
Aspen Plus\cite{aspen} let an engineer assemble a model quickly, but only for the unit operations
and phenomena that their libraries already cover. For a process outside those libraries, the
engineer surveys a large body of papers, textbooks, and handbooks, collects the equations
scattered across them, and refines a prototype model by trial and error. Modeling a new process
is therefore slow, and it depends on the few experts who can read the literature of the field.

To free engineers from this work, our group aims to develop an automated physical model builder,
AutoPMoB\cite{AutoPMoBCSTR,AutoPMoBProto}. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(a) shows its pipeline:
AutoPMoB collects documents about a target process, extracts equations, variables, and
experimental data from them, judges which extracted items are equivalent across documents, and
reorganizes the equivalent items into the desired model. Our group has developed its component
technologies step by step: an annotation tool and an extraction algorithm collect variable symbols
from engineering documents\cite{VARAT}, a language model pre-trained on a chemical-engineering
corpus judges whether two variable definitions refer to the same quantity\cite{ProcessBERT}, and
a computer-algebra algorithm judges whether two equation systems are
equivalent\cite{DAEEquiv}.

This study addresses the reorganization step, the highlighted stage of
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(a): given a description of the desired model together with its input and
output variables, present the equations likely to serve in the model, drawn from an equation
database built from many documents (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(b)). The aim is to present equations
that are likely to be usable, not a finished model; the engineer assembles and checks the model
from them. The step is a retrieval problem whose candidates depend on one another. A physical
model is a set of equations from which the output variables can be computed once the input
variables are given, so an equation that contains a variable other than the inputs and outputs is
useful only together with an equation that determines that variable; a set can determine every
output only if, once the inputs are given, it has at least as many equations as unknown variables.
The concentration dynamics of a continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR), for example, need three
equations: the mass-balance ordinary differential equation (ODE) for the concentration, the
reaction-rate equation $r_A = k C_A$, and the Arrhenius equation $k = A\exp(-E/RT)$ for the rate
constant. The ODE alone leaves the rate $r_A$ undetermined, the rate equation alone leaves the rate
constant $k$ undetermined, and only the three together determine the concentration from the
inputs. A method that scores each equation by its similarity to the description cannot see these
dependencies: each of the three equations looks only moderately relevant on its own, an equation
from an unrelated document may look more relevant than any of them, and no similarity score tells
whether a set of equations determines the outputs. Retrieval must therefore evaluate candidate
equations as a set, not one by one.

We propose a two-stage retrieval method whose second stage evaluates each candidate against other
equations. The first stage ranks the whole database by a mixture of text similarity and variable
overlap and keeps the top candidates. The second stage reranks the candidates with a multilayer
perceptron (MLP) that reads ten features per candidate: seven features of the query--equation
pair, such as the text similarity and the fraction of the query variables the equation contains,
and three set-aware features that compare the candidate with a reference set --- the top-ranked
candidates or, in sequential selection, the equations already selected. The set-aware features
ask whether the candidate supplies query variables the reference set still lacks
(complementarity), whether it shares variables with the set (coherence), and whether it comes from
the same field (domain agreement). We further introduce a learned greedy selection that builds the
ranking one equation at a time and trains with teacher forcing, so that training and inference see
the same sequential context. The method presents the top 20 equations of its ranking; whether they
form a model remains for the engineer to check.

To evaluate the method on realistic data, we construct a large equation database and test cases
whose ground truths all meet the input--output and degree-of-freedom requirements that Kato and
Kano formulated for model building\cite{CombineEq}, and we split them so that no document leaks
between training and testing.

The database holds 11,146 equations from 361 sources: a large language model (LLM), Gemini,
extracted 10,476 of them from 329 documents, and Gemini 2.5 Pro generated the other 670 as
reference sets of textbook fundamentals. The 1,732 evaluation cases fall into three families by how
their ground truths were formed --- picked by an LLM, combined from two or three sources, or
synthesized by a program. The 1,000 synthesized cases grow by a program that adds
variable-sharing equations up to a target size and gives each equation an output variable of its
own, so that, once the inputs are given, each unknown has an equation of its own. The 1,732 cases
leave out 91 cross-source cases in which the inputs leave some unknowns undetermined. We assign every source wholly to
training, validation, or testing, stratify the assignment so that the distributions of four case
properties, such as the number of ground-truth equations, match between training and testing, and
choose the constants of the methods on the validation cases. All experiments repeat over 10 random
splits and report the mean and the standard deviation.

Experiments show that the proposed method outperforms classical retrieval and its variants with
stronger text scorers, and the analyses explain why. With a first stage that admits every
ground-truth equation, Recall@20 --- the fraction of the ground-truth equations among the 20
equations presented --- rises from 0.906 for classical retrieval, whose weights the validation cases
choose, to 0.981 in the mixed setting and from 0.825 to 0.966 in the synthesized-only setting, and
mean average precision (MAP) rises from 0.690 to 0.860 and from 0.626 to 0.832, on all 10 splits.
Variants of the classical retrieval with BM25 and with a dense sentence encoder (E5) rank no better
than the original. The gain grows with the numbers of ground-truth equations and input variables.
% source: experiments/kato1020/main.json（table / compare、wins 10）; baselines.json（BM25 0.904・0.819、E5 0.892・0.806）
The analyses locate the cause of the gain in the variables that equations share rather than in
their topic. The features that read variable overlap carry the improvement, and among them the
set-aware features mainly raise the ground-truth equations within the top 20; permuting the
variable-overlap features of the trained model collapses Recall@20 from 0.981 to 0.020, whereas
permuting the topic features leaves it at 0.973. Learned greedy selection raises MAP over static reranking by
$+0.032$ on the cases with eight or more ground-truth equations. A comparison with LLMs that read the whole
database is prepared and remains to be run.

The contributions of this thesis are threefold.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A benchmark: an 11,146-equation database from 361 sources; 1,732 evaluation cases whose
    ground truths all meet the first three requirements of Kato and Kano and let each unknown be
    assigned to an equation of its own, including 1,000 synthesized cases; and a stratified
    source-disjoint split with 10 seeds, whose validation cases choose the constants.
  \item A set-aware two-stage retrieval method that improves Recall@20 by $+0.075$ and $+0.141$ and
    MAP by $+0.170$ and $+0.205$ over classical information retrieval with weights chosen on the
    validation cases, on all 10 splits, and that also beats the variants with BM25 and E5.
  \item An empirical account of the method --- which features cause the improvement, where
    sequential selection helps, why topical similarity adds little, and where the remaining errors
    arise.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~2 reviews the component technologies of
AutoPMoB, equation discovery from numerical data, information retrieval and reranking, direct
generation by LLMs, and mathematical information retrieval, and states how this study differs from
each. Chapter~3 formulates the task and presents the two-stage method and its sequential variants.
Chapter~4 describes the equation database, the construction of the test cases, and the stratified
split. Chapter~5 defines the evaluation settings, the compared methods and their constants, and the
metrics. Chapter~6 reports the results and discusses them in the order performance, case
characteristics, sources of the improvement, sequential selection, the null result for topic
similarity, error analysis, and limitations. Chapter~7 concludes the thesis and outlines future
work.
