Physical models --- systems of equations that describe how the material, energy, and momentum
of a process change --- underpin the process industry. Process design sizes equipment on them,
process control tunes controllers on them, and a digital twin keeps such a model in step with the
running plant\cite{DigitalTwin}. Purpose-built tools such as MATLAB/Simulink\cite{simulink} and
Aspen Plus\cite{aspen} let an engineer assemble a model quickly, but only for the unit operations
and phenomena that their libraries already cover. For a process outside those libraries, the
engineer surveys a large body of papers, textbooks, and handbooks, collects the equations
scattered across them, and refines a prototype model by trial and error. Modeling a new process
is therefore slow, and it depends on the few experts who can read the literature of the field.

To free engineers from this work, our group aims to develop an automated physical model builder,
AutoPMoB\cite{AutoPMoBCSTR,AutoPMoBProto}. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(a) shows its pipeline:
AutoPMoB collects documents about a target process, extracts equations, variables, and
experimental data from them, judges which extracted items are equivalent across documents, and
reorganizes the equivalent items into the desired model. Our group has developed its component
technologies step by step: an annotation tool and an extraction algorithm collect variable symbols
from engineering documents\cite{VARAT}, a language model pre-trained on a chemical-engineering
corpus judges whether two variable definitions refer to the same quantity\cite{ProcessBERT}, and
a computer-algebra algorithm judges whether two equation systems are
equivalent\cite{DAEEquiv}.

This study addresses the reorganization step, the highlighted stage of
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(a): given a description of the desired model together with its input and
output variables, present the equations likely to serve in the model, drawn from an equation
database built from many documents (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}(b)). The aim is to present equations
that are likely to be usable, not a finished model; the engineer assembles and checks the model
from them. The step is a retrieval problem whose candidates depend on one another. A physical
model is a set of equations from which the output variables can be computed once the input
variables are given, so an equation that contains a variable other than the inputs and outputs is
useful only together with an equation that determines that variable; a set can determine every
output only if, once the inputs are given, it has at least as many equations as unknown variables.
The concentration dynamics of a continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR), for example, need three
equations: the mass-balance ordinary differential equation (ODE) for the concentration, the
reaction-rate equation $r_A = k C_A$, and the Arrhenius equation $k = A\exp(-E/RT)$ for the rate
constant. The ODE alone leaves the rate $r_A$ undetermined, the rate equation alone leaves the rate
constant $k$ undetermined, and only the three together determine the concentration from the
inputs. A method that scores each equation by its similarity to the description cannot see these
dependencies: each of the three equations looks only moderately relevant on its own, an equation
from an unrelated document may look more relevant than any of them, and no similarity score tells
whether a set of equations determines the outputs. Retrieval must therefore evaluate candidate
equations as a set, not one by one.

We propose a two-stage retrieval method whose second stage evaluates each candidate against the
equations already selected. The first stage ranks the whole database by a mixture of text
similarity and variable overlap and keeps the top candidates. The second stage reranks the
candidates with a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that reads ten features per candidate: seven
features of the query--equation pair, such as the text similarity and the fraction of the query
variables the equation contains, and three set-aware features that compare the candidate with a
reference set --- the top-ranked candidates or, in the sequential variants, the equations already
selected. The set-aware features ask whether the candidate supplies query variables the reference
set still lacks (complementarity), whether it shares variables with the set (coherence), and
whether it comes from the same field (domain agreement). We further introduce a learned greedy
selection that builds the set one equation at a time and trains with teacher forcing so that
training and inference see the same sequential context, and a self-terminating criterion
that halts retrieval once the selected set covers every output variable with no more unknowns than
equations (DoF-stop, named after the degrees of freedom it monitors). The method thus ranks the
equations and decides by itself where to cut the ranking, without being told how many equations the
answer has; whether the presented equations form a model remains for the engineer to check.

To evaluate the method on realistic data, we construct a large equation database and test cases
whose evaluation ground truths all meet the input--output and degree-of-freedom requirements that
Kato and Kano formulated for model building\cite{CombineEq}, and we split them so that no document
leaks between training and testing.

The database holds 11,146 equations from 361 sources: large language models (LLMs)
extracted 10,476 of them from 329 documents, and one of them generated the other 670 as reference
sets of textbook fundamentals. The 2,838 cases fall into three families by how their answer sets
were formed --- picked by an LLM within one source, combined across two or three sources, or
synthesized by rule --- plus augmented copies used only for training; the 1,000 synthesized
cases grow by a rule that adds variable-sharing equations up to a target size and gives each
equation an output variable of its own, so that, once the inputs are given, each unknown has an
equation of its own. We assign every source wholly to training, validation, or testing, and we
stratify the assignment so that the distributions of four case properties, such as the number of
ground-truth equations, match between training and testing. All experiments repeat over 10 random
splits and report the mean and the standard deviation.

Experiments show that the proposed method outperforms lexical and dense retrieval baselines and
direct generation by an LLM, and the analyses explain why. Recall@K --- the fraction of the
ground-truth equations found within the top $K$ of the ranking, where $K$ is the size of the
ground truth --- rises from 0.607 for classical retrieval at its best weights to 0.743 in the
mixed setting and from 0.559 to 0.689 in the synthesized-only setting, whose cases hold the most
equations, on all 10 splits (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $p = 0.00195$); a variable-weighted first
stage raises it further to 0.780 and 0.740.

The analyses locate the cause of the gain in the structure of the answer rather than in its
topic: the set-aware features that read shared variables --- complementarity and coherence ---
carry the set-aware gain, domain agreement adds nothing ($p = 0.98$), and permuting the
variable-overlap features of the trained model collapses its score while permuting the topic
features leaves it unchanged.

Two interventions then confirm the mechanism causally. The mechanism analysis predicted that a
fixed reference set saturates on the cases with the most equations, and learned greedy selection
recovers exactly there ($+0.056$ on cases with eight or more equations); the error analysis predicted that the
text-heavy first stage caps what the reranker can reach, and a variable-weighted first stage
raises the candidate coverage and, with it, Recall@K. Without being told the answer size, DoF-stop
stops at the same exact-match rate as selection that is told it (0.526 in the mixed setting and
0.368 in the synthesized-only setting).

The contributions of this thesis are threefold.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A benchmark: an 11,146-equation database from 361 sources, 1,823 evaluation cases whose
    ground truths all meet the first three requirements of Kato and Kano, including 1,000
    synthesized cases in which each unknown has an equation of its own, and a stratified
    source-disjoint split with 10 seeds.
  \item A set-aware two-stage retrieval method that improves Recall@K by $+0.13$ to $+0.18$ over
    classical information retrieval at its best weights, exceeds BM25, dense retrieval, and LLM
    direct generation, and, with a self-terminating criterion, stops by itself without knowing the
    answer size.
  \item An empirical account of the mechanism --- which features cause the improvement, where
    it saturates, and where the remaining errors arise --- confirmed causally by two
    interventions, learned greedy selection and a variable-weighted first stage.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~2 reviews the component technologies of
AutoPMoB, equation discovery from numerical data, information retrieval and reranking, direct
generation by LLMs, and mathematical information retrieval, and states how this study differs from
each. Chapter~3 formulates the task
and presents the two-stage method, its sequential variants, and DoF-stop. Chapter~4 describes the
equation database, the construction of the test cases, and the stratified split. Chapter~5
defines the evaluation settings, the compared methods, and the metrics. Chapter~6 reports the
results and discusses them in the order performance, case characteristics, mechanism, a first
intervention, self-termination, the null result for topic similarity, error analysis, a second
intervention, and limitations. Chapter~7 concludes the thesis and outlines future work.
