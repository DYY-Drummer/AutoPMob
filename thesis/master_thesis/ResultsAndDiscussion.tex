\label{sec:overall}

Recall@K of the best-weighted classical baseline, the proposed method, and LLM direct generation.

The secondary metrics qualify the gain. In MAP the proposed method with the weights $(0.7, 0.3)$
leads the best baseline on all 10 splits, by $+0.104$ in Setting~A and $+0.088$ in Setting~B

$(0.7, 0.3)$ nearly ties the best baseline: 0.909 against 0.907 in Setting~A ($p = 0.49$) and 0.838

and under these weights the candidates hold only 92.0\% of the ground-truth equations in
Setting~A. The weights $(0.3, 0.7)$ raise this coverage to 96.7\% and Recall@20 to 0.957 and 0.925.

LLM direct generation stays far below the retrieval methods. With equivalence-based scoring it
reaches Recall@K of 0.254 in Setting~A ($n = 48$), 0.226 in Setting~B ($n = 50$), and 0.300 on the
full collection with the augmented cases ($n = 49$). The model wrote 11.6 equations per case on
average, against 3.9 and 5.6 ground-truth equations in the samples of Settings A and B; equivalents
of 41.9\% and 34.3\% of the ground-truth equations appear somewhere among the first 12 written (the
rank-free coverage; 0.567 on the full collection), but only part of them among the first $K$. Under
the second judge, Claude Fable 5.1, which rejects more pairs than the first (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}),
the same generations score 0.198, 0.150, and 0.251 (coverage 0.338, 0.234, and 0.463), so the
choice of judge moves the level but not the conclusion. This comparison evaluates about 50 cases
per setting once; evaluating every case, repeating the evaluation, and comparing with a language
model that reads the source documents remain future work.

Stronger text scorers do not close the gap either. Alone, every text scorer ranks poorly:
Recall@K is 0.120 for TF-IDF, 0.150 for BM25, and 0.146 for E5 in Setting~A, and 0.153, 0.164,
and 0.178 in Setting~B, so the description alone does not identify the equations of a case,
whereas the variable-overlap term alone reaches 0.603 and 0.541.

Setting~B and E5 reaches 0.602 and 0.544 (0.598 and 0.547 when the query omits the variable
symbols), no better than the TF-IDF baseline at its best weights (0.607 and 0.559).

The best mixtures agree to within 0.02 and exceed the variable term alone by at most $+0.004$
in Setting~A and $+0.018$ in Setting~B, so the scorers are interchangeable inside the mixture:

$(0.7, 0.3)$ exceeds the best BM25 and E5 mixtures by $+0.142$ and $+0.141$ in Setting~A and by
$+0.134$ and $+0.145$ in Setting~B, on all 10 seeds (Wilcoxon $p = 0.00195$; $d_z = 5.7$ and 6.2 in

mixture covers more than 0.965 of the ground truth at $k = 50$ in Setting~A, below the 0.967 of
the variable-weighted TF-IDF window of Section~\ref{sec:stage1redesign}.

Neither classical retrieval, a stronger text scorer, nor direct generation solves the task alone
--- the retrieval baselines rank equations by query--equation relevance only, and the language
model composes plausible equations without provenance --- and the rest of this chapter explains
where and why the proposed combination works.

\section{Mechanism: Which Features Cause the Improvement}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The gain of $+0.222$ in Setting~A over the baseline with the same first-stage weights $(0.7, 0.3)$
decomposes into three steps, which we measure by growing the feature set of the reranker and
retraining at each step.
% source: experiments/xd/（10 seeds; reranker-2 0.6010, reranker-2+Spec 0.6908（+0.0898,
%         p_wilcoxon=0.00195）, reranker-2+InCov 0.6203, reranker-2+OutCov 0.6071,
%         reranker-2+Dom 0.6034, reranker-2+Svd 0.5989, reranker-7 0.6934, reranker-10S 0.7429）
First, learning alone: a reranker that sees only the two Stage-1 features but learns their
weighting (reranker-2) moves Recall@K from 0.521 to 0.601 ($+0.080$). Second, per-equation
features: growing the input to the seven features of Table~\ref{tab:features} adds another
$+0.092$ (0.601 to 0.693), and one feature carries almost all of it --- adding specificity alone
reaches 0.691 ($+0.090$, Wilcoxon $p = 0.00195$), while adding the domain match or the latent
similarity alone changes the score by $+0.002$ and $-0.002$ (Section~\ref{sec:nullresult}).
Third, the set-aware features: the step from reranker-7 to reranker-10S adds the remaining
$+0.050$ (Wilcoxon $p = 0.00195$, $d_z = 4.7$).
% source: experiments/feature_x_difficulty_stats.json overall_tests.reranker-10S
The set-aware contribution is therefore real but modest, and most of the improvement comes from
the reranker learning one lesson: an equation whose variables are specific to the requested
model probably belongs to it.

Which of the three set-aware features carries the $+0.050$? Adding each alone to reranker-7
answers directly: complementarity adds $+0.052$ and coherence $+0.050$ (each Wilcoxon
$p = 0.00195$), while domain agreement adds $-0.0001$ ($p = 0.98$).
% source: experiments/feature_x_difficulty_stats.json overall_tests（+Comp 0.0518 / +Coh 0.0497 /
%         +Dom -0.0001）
The two structural features are individually sufficient and largely redundant with each other ---
all three together add no more than one of them alone --- and the topical one contributes nothing,
a null result that Section~\ref{sec:nullresult} traces to its cause.

Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism} shows where the set-aware lift lives. Across the number of ground-truth
equations it has a threshold-and-hump shape: nothing on single-equation cases ($+0.002$, where no
reference set can add information), a peak at three equations ($+0.102$), and a settled $+0.02$
beyond. Across the number of input variables the lift climbs to $+0.078$ at 11--15 variables and
then drops to $+0.035$ at 16 or more.
% source: experiments/feature_x_difficulty_stats.json lift_by_n_correct / lift_by_n_input
%         （reranker-10S; X=1 +0.0022, X=3 +0.1018; 11-15 +0.0782, 16+ +0.0348）
The cases with the most input variables thus profit only half as much as those with 11--15. We
attribute this saturation to the static reference set: during static scoring the set-aware features
are computed once against the Stage-1 top five (Section~\ref{sec:stage2}), and in a long answer that
fixed reference resembles the true partial answer less and less as selection proceeds. The
attribution is testable --- a method that rebuilds the reference set during selection should recover
the loss on the cases with many equations --- and Section~\ref{sec:greedyresults} runs exactly that
intervention.

Ablation retrains the model for every feature set, so it measures \emph{necessity}: whether the
performance is recoverable without a feature. Permutation feature importance (PFI) asks the
complementary question --- how much the single trained model \emph{relies} on each feature --- by
permuting one feature's values across the candidates of each case and measuring the drop in
Recall@K, with no retraining.
% source: experiments/pfi_results.json results.case（10 seeds × 20 permutations; one-sample
%         t-test over seeds; GROUP_var +0.678 p<1e-4, GROUP_topic +0.0064 p=0.10,
%         specificity +0.617（0.7429 − 0.6168 = 0.126）; 話題の 4 特徴量は個別に p ≥ 0.23; global 置換 GROUP_var +0.675）
Fig.~\ref{fig:pfi} shows Recall@K after the permutation for reranker-10S. Permuting the six
variable-overlap features together collapses Recall@K from 0.743 to 0.065 (a drop of 0.678;
one-sample $t$-test of the drop over the 10 splits, $p < 10^{-4}$), whereas permuting the four topic
features together leaves it at 0.737 (a drop of 0.006, $p = 0.10$). Permuting specificity alone
lowers Recall@K to 0.126, the largest single-feature drop (0.617); no topic feature lowers it
significantly on its own, and permuting across the whole test set instead of within each case gives
the same group drop (0.675 against 0.678), so the finding does not depend on the permutation scope.

The two analyses disagree in one instructive place, and the disagreement is informative rather
than troubling. Specificity's reliance under PFI ($+0.617$) far exceeds its ablation gain over
reranker-2 ($+0.090$), because the two numbers answer different questions: ablation retrains, and
a retrained model recovers the variable-overlap signal from the remaining features of the group;
PFI freezes the weights, and the frozen model cannot re-route around a corrupted input. Read
together, they say that the variable-overlap \emph{signal} is necessary while the particular
feature carrying it is substitutable within the group. Where it matters, the two methods agree:
variable overlap carries the improvement, and topic carries none.

\section{Intervention: Learned Greedy Selection}
\label{sec:greedyresults}

Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} predicted that rebuilding the reference set during selection should
recover the saturation on the cases with many equations. We test the prediction with the three
selection strategies of Section~\ref{sec:seq}, mainly in Setting~B because its cases have the most
ground-truth equations. Sequential selection changes only how the second stage orders the
candidates, so these experiments widen the Stage-1 window to $k = 200$, which lifts the
candidate coverage from 92.0\% to 97.8\% (Section~\ref{sec:erroranalysis}) and lets differences in
ordering show; their static values therefore exceed the $k = 50$ values of Table~\ref{tab:overall}
and are comparable only within this section.
% top-k: run_greedy_3way.sh / run_greedy_3way_A.sh / run_dof_stop.sh いずれも TOPK=200
% source: experiments/greedy_3way_stats.json overall / tests（static 0.7237 < infer 0.7556 <
%         train 0.7649; infer_vs_static +0.0319 p_w=0.00195 dz=3.28;

0.756 $<$ learned greedy 0.765 over the same 10 seeds. Greedy selection at inference alone
recovers $+0.032$ (Wilcoxon $p = 0.00195$, $d_z = 3.3$), and training on the sequential contexts
adds a further $+0.009$ ($p = 0.037$, $d_z = 0.78$): a small increment with a clear reading ---
training must ask the same question as inference, namely which equation to select next, given the
ones already chosen.

The gain concentrates exactly where Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} located the saturation. On cases
with eight or more ground-truth equations, learned greedy beats static selection by $+0.056$
($p = 0.00195$, $d_z = 3.7$) and beats inference-only greedy by $+0.013$ ($p = 0.020$), its

%         +0.0132 p_w=0.0195）
Setting~A repeats the pattern at smaller scale: static 0.757 $<$ inference greedy 0.787 $<$

\section{Self-terminating Retrieval}
\label{sec:dofstopresults}

Every experiment so far told the method how many equations to return. DoF-stop removes that help:
it runs the same learned greedy selector and stops at the first prefix that meets the stopping
conditions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stopcond}. We compare it against the oracle that cuts the same ranking
at the true answer size.
% source: experiments/dof_stop_stats.json（learned greedy + stop-dof, top_k=200, 10 seeds）
Withholding the answer size costs almost nothing (Fig.~\ref{fig:dof}). The exact-match rate of
DoF-stop equals the oracle's not only on average but case by case --- 0.526 in Setting~A and
0.368 in Setting~B, with zero disagreements among the 7,026 per-case records --- because whenever
the first $K$ selections were exactly the ground truth, the stopping conditions held for the first
time at the $K$-th selection.
% source: experiments/xd_dof/ per_case 全レコードで set_exact_dof == set_exact_oracleK（不一致 0）
The set-$F_1$ cost is $-0.008$ in Setting~A and $-0.023$ in Setting~B (paired $t$-test,
$p = 0.006$ and $p = 0.0001$): measurable and small.
% source: dof_stop_stats.json dof_vs_oracle_f1

The stop-condition rate is 0.936 for DoF-stop against 0.865 for the oracle in Setting~A, and 0.883
against 0.781 in Setting~B. The higher rate follows from the rule itself, which keeps selecting
until the conditions hold, and meeting the conditions does not make a set solvable
(Section~\ref{sec:dofstop}).
% source: dof_stop_stats.json closed_dof / closed_oracleK（A 0.9363 / 0.8651, B 0.8826 / 0.7806）
DoF-stop does not always meet the conditions: 6.4\% of its returned sets in Setting~A and 11.7\% in
Setting~B never met them within the selection cap of 20 equations. All of these records are
synthesized cases, nine in ten of them with six or more equations, and in 98\% (Setting~A) and 99\%
(Setting~B) of them the first stage had left at least one answer equation out of the candidates; no
selection order can recover an equation that is not among them.
% source: experiments/xd_dof/ per_case（closed_dof = 0 は A 297/4,594・B 295/2,432 件。すべて k_stop = 20
%         の合成ケースで、X >= 6 が 93\%（A）・92\%（B）。正解式に候補外（rank 10,000）を含むのは 291 件ずつ
%         = 98.0\%・98.6\%）。6.4\%・11.7\% は乱数ごとの割合の平均（1 − closed_dof）。
The size errors are one-sided in practice: the median size error is zero, every erring record in
Setting~B overshoots (545 of 2,432), and in Setting~A every erring record but one does; the records
that ran to the cap account for 295 and 297 of these overshoots. When DoF-stop misses the size, it
has kept selecting past the answer rather than stopped short of it (mean size error $+1.7$
equations in Setting~B).
% source: dof_stop_stats.json（K_median_abs_err 0.0; K_bias 1.7212 = K_MAE（B）, 0.9293 vs
%         0.9297（A）; per_case 実測: B は k_pred_dof < n_correct が 0/2,432、A は 1/4,594）

One property of the dataset keeps this claim honest. In our cases the answer size nearly equals
the number of output variables (Section~4.2), so predicting the size itself would be easy, and we
claim no difficulty there. What DoF-stop demonstrates is that the retriever can decide by itself
when to stop and still match the oracle's exact-match rate; it does not demonstrate that the set at
which it stops forms a solvable model. Searching the candidates for a combination that meets all
four requirements, as the combination method of Kato and Kano does\cite{CombineEq}, and checking
that each unknown can be matched to an equation of its own are left to future work
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

%   停止条件の充足率が高いのは規則の定義による。加藤先生の注釈 #33）

\section{The Null Result for Topic Similarity}
\label{sec:nullresult}

Four of the ten features read topical closeness: the text similarity $f_1$, the latent similarity
$f_3$, the domain match $f_7$, and the set-level domain agreement $g_{\mathrm{Dom}}$
(Table~\ref{tab:features}). None of them improves reranking in any form we tried. The result
deserves this section because the features rest on a reasonable expectation --- the equations of
one model should share a topic --- and because finding where the expectation breaks tells us what
signal the task actually rewards.

The failure spans every encoding of topic. Adding the domain match to reranker-2 moves Recall@K
by $+0.002$ (paired $t$-test $p = 0.063$, Wilcoxon $p = 0.123$); adding the latent similarity
instead moves it by $-0.002$ ($p = 0.48$); adding the set-level domain agreement to reranker-7
moves it by $-0.0001$ ($p = 0.98$; Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}); and in the trained ten-feature
model, permuting all four topic features together lowers Recall@K by only 0.006 ($p = 0.10$),
whereas permuting the variable-overlap group collapses it.
% source: experiments/xd/（reranker-2 0.6010 → +Dom 0.6034 p_t=0.063 / +Svd 0.5989 p_t=0.48）,
%         feature_x_difficulty_stats.json overall_tests.reranker-7+Dom,
%         pfi_results.json results.case GROUP_topic

An obvious suspect is the label vocabulary. The domain labels are free text written by the
extracting language models, and Section~4.1 measured the consequence: 1,998 distinct labels over the 11,146
equations, 27.9\% occurring exactly once, 6.36 labels within an average source, and
near-duplicates such as ``Transport Phenomena'' and ``Transport phenomena'' counted as distinct.
Both domain features therefore fire sparsely. Merging the labels tests this suspicion directly:
normalized to 1,958 groups, or clustered into 100 or 40 topical groups --- the scale of the
collection taxonomy --- the domain match adds at most $+0.0006$ (paired $t$-test, all $p > 0.6$),
and even the $+0.002$ of the raw labels disappears.
% source: experiments/domain_merge_stats.json（L1 +0.0006 p_t=0.68; K100 -0.0001 p_t=0.84;
%         K40 +0.0004 p_t=0.63; run_domain_merge_ablation.py）
A finer taxonomy would deepen the sparsity, and a coarser one does not help either, so sparsity is
not the obstacle.

The aggregate zero of the domain agreement is, moreover, a balance rather than an absence. We
split the evaluation cases by whether the feature happens to separate toward the answer within
that case's candidates, counting each case once (its permutation drop averaged over the seeds in
which it appears, which avoids treating the same case as ten observations). Where the feature
separates toward the answer (1,169 cases), permuting it costs 0.012 of Recall@K; where it
separates away (414 cases), permuting it \emph{gains} 0.034; the two groups differ (Mann--Whitney
$p = 1.3 \times 10^{-8}$), and their weighted sum reproduces the observed zero ($-0.0002$).
% source: experiments/pfi_profile_stats.json features.gDom.cancellation_all_cases
%         （+0.0119 (n=1,169) / -0.0342 (n=414); 加重和 (1169*0.0119-414*0.0342)/1583 = -0.0002;
%         残る 9 ケースは sep=0。analyze_pfi_profile.py collapse_to_cases でケース集約済み）
The trained model does listen to the label case by case; the label misleads it often enough to
cancel the cases where it helps. Any future use of this feature must first make the label point
the right way more often --- and the merging experiment shows that vocabulary cleanup alone does
not.

The latent similarity fails for a different and more instructive reason, which a diagnosis
requiring no trained model locates (Fig.~\ref{fig:topicdiag}). A natural hypothesis says the
TF-IDF--SVD projection crowds the query vectors together until cases become indistinguishable.
Panel~(a) rejects it: the cosine similarity between the query vectors of two cases has median
0.218 in the SVD space and 0.070 in the raw TF-IDF space, and it exceeds 0.5 for only 5.4\% of
pairs.
% source: experiments/topic_feature_diagnosis.json A_case_vector_similarity（SVD median 0.2178,
%         raw 0.0702, share>0.5 5.4%; 200,000 sampled pairs）
Decisively, the latent similarity alone separates the ground-truth equations from the rest of the
11,146-equation database with an area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) of
0.950 (case mean; standard error 0.003) --- impossible if the vectors had collapsed. The signal
exists; it is spent in the wrong place. Panel~(b) compares each feature's AUC against the whole
database with its AUC inside the top-50 candidates: the latent similarity falls from 0.950 to
0.594 and the text similarity from 0.913 to 0.437, while specificity holds 0.965 and the I/O
Jaccard 0.947.
% source: topic_feature_diagnosis.json D_full_db_auc（svd 0.950±0.003, text 0.913±0.003,
%         io_jaccard 0.999）/ C_within_candidate_auc（svd 0.594±0.006, text 0.437±0.007,
%         specificity 0.965±0.001, io_jaccard 0.947±0.002）
Stage~1 has already done the coarse screening that topical similarity is good at, so the
surviving candidates agree on topic and differ on variables; what remains to be discriminated,
topic does not discriminate.

Candidate selection even reverses the surface text similarity. Stage~1 admits an equation for a
weighted sum of text similarity and variable overlap (Eq.~\eqref{eq:stage1}), so inside the
admitted set the two ingredients correlate at $-0.404$: an admitted equation with high text
similarity tends to be one that needed the text term to get in, that is, one whose variables
match poorly.
% source: experiments/case_outcomes_seed42.json selection_effect_corr（corr(text_sim,io_jaccard)
%         = -0.404, corr(text_sim,specificity) = -0.392; seed 42, 423 cases）
Because the answers are decided on the variable side, the text similarity points slightly away
from them within the candidates (AUC 0.437). A two-stage design therefore spends topical
similarity exactly once, in Stage~1, and gains nothing by spending it twice.

\section{Error Analysis: Where the Remaining Errors Come From}
\label{sec:erroranalysis}

The proposed method still misses about a quarter of the ground-truth equations in Setting~A, and
this section locates the misses. The candidate window bounds the reranker from above: over the
1,823 evaluation cases and under the first-stage weights $(0.7, 0.3)$, the top $k = 50$ candidates
contain 92.0\% of the ground-truth equations, and all of them for only 77.2\% of the cases; at
$k = 200$ the two numbers rise to 97.8\% and 90.3\%.
% source: 実測（scratch: coverage_by_k.py, 設定A 1,823件）k=50 0.9203/0.772, k=100 0.9569/0.856,
%         k=200 0.9775/0.903, k=400 0.9895/0.947（2026-10-12 に §3.2 から移した。結果は手法の節に書かない）
We decompose the misses of one split (seed 42): its 423 test cases hold 1,579 ground-truth
equations, of which 435, in 211 cases, are missing from the top $K$. Of these 435 equations, 166
(38.2\%) had entered the candidate set and ranked below the cutoff, and the other 269 (61.8\%) never
entered it. At $k = 50$, the first stage, not the reranker, causes the larger share of the residual
error.
% source: experiments/case_outcomes_seed42.json failure_decomposition（435 = 269 + 166）, outcome_counts
%         （212 solved / 211 partial_or_failed）; 正解式 1,579 本は strat_A.json per_case（seed 42）の n_correct の和
Widening the window moves this balance without removing the errors: at $k = 200$ the same split
misses 373 equations, of which 267 (71.6\%) had entered the candidates and 106 (28.4\%) had not, so
ranking errors dominate. The first stage therefore bounds the $k = 50$ configuration of
Table~\ref{tab:overall}, while the sequential experiments of Sections \ref{sec:greedyresults} and
\ref{sec:dofstopresults}, which already run at $k = 200$, are bounded by the reranker.
% source: experiments/case_outcomes_seed42_k200.json failure_decomposition（373 = 106 + 267,
%         stage1_share 0.284; Recall@K 0.791→0.807）

The Stage-1 blend itself sets part of the bound. Ranking the database by variable overlap alone
covers 96.1\% of the ground-truth equations within 50 candidates, against 92.0\% for the blend
$(0.7, 0.3)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stage1}: at the margin, the text term costs coverage for the same
reason it fails inside the window (Section~\ref{sec:nullresult}).
% source: topic_feature_diagnosis.json E_stage1_coverage（io_jaccard_only 0.961±0.002 vs
%         blend 0.920±0.004）
This measurement predicts that a variable-weighted first stage would raise the ceiling;
Section~\ref{sec:stage1redesign} runs that intervention and confirms it.

Failed cases differ from solved cases in size, not in kind. They average 5.34 ground-truth
equations against 2.13 and 17.8 input variables against 10.8 (Mann--Whitney, both
$p < 10^{-14}$), and their candidate coverage is 0.83 against 1.00.
% source: case_outcomes_seed42.json solved_vs_unsolved_attrs（212 solved / 211 unsolved）
For the misses that do survive into the candidate set, no present feature would repair the
ranking: pairing each missed equation with each wrong equation ranked above it, the missed one
has the higher latent similarity in 0.516 of the pairs (chance level), the higher domain
agreement in 0.494, and the higher variable overlap in only 0.375 --- the wrong equation usually
looks at least as good on every feature the model has.
% source: case_outcomes_seed42.json topic_rescue_fraction（131 cases with reranker misses）

Two concrete error types make up that residue. First, standard physical equations recur across
documents, so the model may pick an identical equation from another source: in one
batch-fermentation case the answer $\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = \mu X$ lost to the same growth
equation printed in another document, which the topic features scored \emph{higher}. The
evaluation identifies equations by their identifiers, so it counts such a pick as incorrect,
although the engineer receives the same, usable equation; 386 of the 3,132 ground-truth equations
have an identical twin in another source (Section~4.2), and the Recall@K of every retrieval method
is lower by this type of error. Second, the features read only variable sets, so two equations that
determine different variables from the same variables are indistinguishable: the paired equations
for $\mathrm{d}C_A'/\mathrm{d}t$ and $\mathrm{d}T'/\mathrm{d}t$ of one linearized reactor tie on
every feature, and the wrong one can rank first. This error misleads the engineer as well, and its
natural repair is a feature that encodes which variable an equation determines; neither error type
yields to more topical information.
% source: docs/考察_特徴量の期待と結果_2026-08-09.md §5.5（dae_X1_040, dae_X1_089）;
%         kato_0831_followup_stats.json gt_checks.identical_latex_in_other_source（386 / 3,132）

\section{Second Intervention: A Variable-weighted First Stage}
\label{sec:stage1redesign}

Section~\ref{sec:erroranalysis} located the larger share of the residual error before the
reranker runs and predicted that weighting the first stage toward variables would raise the
ceiling. This section runs that intervention. We vary the first-stage weights
$(w_{\mathrm{t}}, w_{\mathrm{v}})$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stage1} from $(0.7, 0.3)$ to $(0.5, 0.5)$,
$(0.3, 0.7)$, and $(0.0, 1.0)$ and repeat the whole pipeline --- candidate retrieval, training, and
evaluation all under the same weights --- over the same 10 stratified splits of Setting~A.
% source: run_stage1_weights.sh（3重み × baseline/reranker-10S × 10 seed, k=50）。実装は
%         set_aware_reranker.py --stage1-w-text/--stage1-w-var。既定重みの再実行が正典
%         experiments/xd/reranker-10S__42.json を per-case 423 件まで完全再現することを
%         確認した上で、w70-30 参照には既存の正典（xd/, strat_A.json）を用いた。

The ceiling moves as predicted, and its best position is more informative than the prediction
was. Fig.~\ref{fig:stage1redesign}(a) sweeps the candidate coverage over the mixing weights: at
$k = 50$ the coverage rises from 0.920 under the weights $(0.7, 0.3)$ to 0.961 with the variable
term alone --- the value Section~\ref{sec:erroranalysis} anticipated --- and to 0.967 at
$(0.3, 0.7)$, which is the best mixture at every window size.
% source: experiments/stage1_weight_coverage.json（k=50: w70-30 0.9203 / w50-50 0.9608 /
%         w30-70 0.9671 / w00-100 0.9610）
Pure variable ranking is therefore not the optimum: a text term kept at low weight still pays at
the margin, so the correct repair keeps both ingredients and inverts their priority.

The raised ceiling converts into an end-metric gain of about the same size
(Fig.~\ref{fig:stage1redesign}(b)). Retrained on the $(0.3, 0.7)$ candidates, reranker-10S
reaches Recall@K 0.780 against 0.743 under the weights $(0.7, 0.3)$ ($+0.037$, Wilcoxon
$p = 0.00195$, $d_z = 2.4$, all 10 seeds improved), and the four weightings order the end metric
exactly as they order the coverage: 0.743, 0.766, 0.780, and 0.776 for text weights 0.7, 0.5, 0.3,
and 0.0.
% source: experiments/stage1_weight_stats.json overall.reranker-10S / tests（+0.0234 dz=1.37 /
%         +0.0370 dz=2.37 / +0.0333 dz=1.78, いずれも p_wilcoxon=0.00195）
Roughly 80\% of the added ceiling ($+0.047$) reaches the final ranking ($+0.037$). The
conclusion does not hang on picking the best mixture: every variable-leaning weight beats
$(0.7, 0.3)$ on all 10 seeds. The gain concentrates where Section~\ref{sec:erroranalysis} located
the bottleneck --- on cases with eight or more ground-truth equations, whose depressed candidate
coverage was the signature of failure, the $(0.3, 0.7)$ first stage adds $+0.094$
($p = 0.00195$). In Setting~B the weights $(0.3, 0.7)$ likewise raise Recall@K from 0.689 to 0.740
($+0.051$, all 10 splits, $p = 0.00195$).
% source: stage1_weight_stats.json tests_hard_X8plus（w50-50 +0.0740 / w30-70 +0.0937 /
%         w00-100 +0.0854）; kato_0831_followup_stats.json stage1_B_w30-70.vs_w70-30（+0.0511, wins 10,
%         p 0.00195。run_stage1_weights_B.sh, experiments/xs1_B/）

Part of the improvement needs no learning at all: the classical baseline itself rises from 0.521
to about 0.60 under every variable-leaning weight, which quantifies how much the weights
$(0.7, 0.3)$ under-used the variable signal. The reranker keeps its margin of about $+0.18$ over
the baseline evaluated with the same weights, so the learned second stage and the redesigned first
stage improve different parts of the pipeline.
% source: stage1_weight_stats.json overall.baseline（w50-50 0.6016 / w30-70 0.5998 /
%         w00-100 0.6032; いずれも vs 0.5206 で p_wilcoxon=0.00195）

The redesign carries into the strongest configuration. Under learned greedy selection at
$k = 200$ --- the configuration of Sections \ref{sec:greedyresults} and \ref{sec:dofstopresults}
--- the $(0.3, 0.7)$ first stage lifts Recall@K from 0.797 to 0.810 ($+0.013$, Wilcoxon
$p = 0.0098$, $d_z = 1.1$, 9 of 10 seeds), and the cases with many equations again carry the gain:
$+0.035$ at eight or more ground-truth equations ($p = 0.00195$).

The smaller increment matches the smaller headroom: at $k = 200$ the coverage gap between the
two mixtures narrows to $+0.015$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:stage1redesign}(a)), and $+0.013$ of it reaches
the final ranking. At both window sizes, then, the reranker converts nearly every additional
correct candidate the first stage admits. What remains after the redesign are the ranking errors
of Section~\ref{sec:erroranalysis}, which call for new features rather than a wider or better
window.

This is the second time the chapter closes a predict--intervene--recover arc: the mechanism
analysis predicted the saturation of the static reference set and learned greedy selection
recovered it (Section~\ref{sec:greedyresults}); the error analysis predicted the ceiling of the
text-heavy first stage, and the variable-weighted first stage recovers it, with the recovery
sized and placed as predicted. The two interventions repair different components, and their
effects arrive where their respective analyses said they would.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Nine limitations qualify these results. First, language models produced parts of the data. One
wrote the descriptions of the synthesized cases, whose style may differ from human modeling
requests, although the equations, inputs, and outputs of those cases follow from the rule-based
construction (Section~4.3). Another picked the answer sets of the 92 single-source cases, which are
the sets it judged minimal rather than the models their sources present (Section~4.2). Second, the
number of sources is confounded with the number of equations, so Section~\ref{sec:bycharacteristics}
draws no causal conclusion from it. Third, the stratified split is one scheme selected from 400
candidates; the 10 seeds average over the randomness within the scheme, not over schemes. We used
no validation split: the hyperparameters stayed fixed, the weights were compared on the test
splits, and the analyses of Sections~\ref{sec:bycharacteristics}--\ref{sec:erroranalysis} use the
first-stage weights $(0.7, 0.3)$ rather than the better $(0.3, 0.7)$. Fourth, the evaluation is
database-internal --- retrieval cannot find an equation absent from the database --- and the
language-model comparison rests on about 50 cases per setting, evaluated once and scored by a
language model's equivalence judgments, which we cross-checked with a second judge (88\% agreement,
$\kappa = 0.72$; Section~\ref{sec:metrics}) but neither verified by hand nor repeated. Fifth, the
retrieval methods are judged by identifiers, so an identical equation from another source counts as
an error (Section~\ref{sec:erroranalysis}), and 91 cross-source ground truths (5.0\%) leave some
unknowns undetermined once the inputs are given (Section~4.2). Sixth, the variable-overlap group
alone is highly predictive in this benchmark: permuting it costs 0.68 of Recall@K
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), so part of the advantage reflects how the cases were constructed ---
every query takes its variables from the symbols of its ground-truth equations (Section~4.2) --- and
transfer to queries whose variable notation differs from the database remains untested. Seventh,
learned greedy trains with teacher forcing: during training the reference set is the true partial
answer, while at inference it contains the model's own mistakes; scheduled sampling is the standard
remedy, and we leave it to future work. Eighth, the external retrieval baselines use BM25 with its
default parameters and one dense encoder (E5-base) without fine-tuning on the training cases; a
retriever trained on this database might score higher than the off-the-shelf encoder, although
every text scorer in Section~\ref{sec:overall} adds at most $+0.02$ over the variable-overlap term,
which points to the task rather than to the encoder. Ninth, DoF-stop checks only the output part of
requirement~1 and a relaxed requirement~2, selects greedily among the first-stage candidates without
backtracking, and checks neither independence nor consistency, so the set at which it stops need not
be solvable (Sections~\ref{sec:dofstop} and~\ref{sec:dofstopresults}).
